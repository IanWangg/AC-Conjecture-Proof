# Round 02 · Ordinary Andrews–Curtis proof

2026-11-19. An ordinary path at a fixed padded rank can be compiled to the original rank when each vertex admits a triangular set of defining relations for the auxiliary generators. The relation slots and the order of elimination can change along the path. This extends the [single-auxiliary projection](round-01-ac-proof.md) and isolates a remaining obstruction to removing arbitrary stabilization.

## Triangular defining relations

Let $F=F(X)$, with $|X|=n$, and $G=F*F(Z)$, with a fixed set $Z$ of $k$ auxiliary basis letters. Consider an $(n+k)$-tuple in $G$. A triangular frame consists of $k$ distinct row slots assigned bijectively to $Z$, with the following properties.

1. The cyclically reduced word in the slot assigned to $z$ has exactly one occurrence of $z$ or $z^{-1}$. It is therefore conjugate, up to inversion, to $z a_z^{-1}$, for a word $a_z$ avoiding $z$.
2. Put an arrow $z\to t$ whenever $t\in Z$ occurs in $a_z^{\pm1}$ as a signed generator. The dependency graph is acyclic.

A pivot row is called pure if the cyclically reduced word has just one auxiliary occurrence in total. A triangular frame always has a pure pivot. Solve its equation first, proceed in any sink-first order of the dependencies, and apply the resulting retraction to the unselected rows. The projected tuple has $n$ entries in $F$. The retraction is unique, since in dependency order the equations determine the value of every auxiliary. This uses only cyclic reduction, solving one-occurrence relations, and substitution. [Fagan, substitution and removal in Lemma 3.2](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6fm617h4/qt6fm617h4.pdf?v=lg).

After individual conjugations and inversions, the selected rows are the elements $z a_z^{-1}$. Together with $X$ they form a free basis of $G$. Indeed, the substitution $\theta(z)=z a_z^{-1}$ fixing $X$ has an inverse defined recursively in dependency order by $\theta^{-1}(z)=z\theta^{-1}(a_z)$. Verification of both compositions proceeds in the same order. In particular, killing the normal closure of any $j$ of the selected rows gives a marked free product of $F$ and a free group of rank $k-j$.

## Frame exchanges

Call a set of $k$ rows a relative-basis frame if its entries can individually be conjugated and inverted so that they extend the marked basis $X$ to a free basis of $G$. The triangular frames above have this property.

**One-row exchange.** Two relative-basis frames at a tuple give ordinarily AC-equivalent projections to $F$ whenever they share $k-1$ rows. Kill the common rows first. The marked quotient is a free product $F*\langle t\rangle$. In this quotient, killing either remaining pivot gives precisely $F$, with its original marking. Write $a\in F$ for the resulting image of $t$ under one of these quotients. Its kernel is $\langle\!\langle t a^{-1}\rangle\!\rangle$. The projected pivot has the same normal closure, so Magnus's theorem shows it is conjugate to $(t a^{-1})^{\pm1}$. The same argument applies to the other pivot. Round 01's one-auxiliary frame exchange now applies to the tuple in the common quotient. The resulting projections are the full-frame projections because the quotients fix the same marked copy of $F$. [Magnus's theorem, as recalled in Bogopolski–Sviridov, Theorem 1.1](https://msp.org/gtm/2008/14/gtm-2008-14-004s.pdf), [round 01, Lemma 1](round-01-ac-proof.md#projection-along-a-path).

**Exchange connectivity for triangular frames.** Any two triangular frames at a fixed tuple are connected by single-row exchanges. Order the second frame from dependencies to dependents. Replace the first frame's pivot for each successive variable with the pivot for that variable in the second frame. At that stage the new row can involve only its assigned auxiliary and the already handled ones. It cannot already occupy a row slot assigned to another unhandled auxiliary, since it contains none of them. The handled slots are already the distinct slots from the second frame, so there is no slot collision. The replacement creates no directed cycle, since the already handled pivots have no arrows to the unhandled variables. There are at most $k$ replacements. Combined with the previous argument, all triangular frame projections at a vertex lie in the same ordinary AC orbit.

This also gives a greedy exact recognition test. At each step use the *original cyclic words* in unused slots and count only letters from the remaining auxiliary set. If some unused row has exactly one such letter, assign that row to its remaining generator and remove the row and generator from the bookkeeping. If a complete frame was possible, the chosen row can be forced into it by the exchange argument, without depending on the unhandled choices. If no candidate remains before the auxiliary set is empty, no triangular frame exists. Substituting already solved words is needed to compute the projection. Recognition uses just the occurrence counts in the original cyclic words.

## Projection of an entire path

**Theorem.** Suppose each vertex of a fixed-rank ordinary AC path in $G$ has a triangular frame relative to the same $X,Z$. Then the projections of its endpoints through any triangular frames are ordinary AC-equivalent in $F$.

**Proof.** By frame exchange it suffices to prove the result for a single edge and any convenient choices at its endpoints. Induct on $k$. For $k=0$ the edge itself suffices. Inversion and conjugation preserve every cyclic one-occurrence condition, and they can keep the same pivot choices. If they act on a pivot, its equation and retraction are unchanged. Otherwise they give the projected ordinary move. A swap just reindexes rows. It remains to consider right multiplication of $r$ by a distinct row $q$, giving $r'=r q$. Signed multiplication follows by temporarily inverting its donor, which preserves frames. [Ordinary AC moves](https://arxiv.org/html/1609.00325v1).

If there is an unchanged pure pivot for $z$, force it into both chosen frames. This is possible by substituting it for each frame's pivot for $z$, since the new row has no other auxiliary and creates no dependency. Project through that row. A pivot for another auxiliary still has its one occurrence of the assigned variable after substituting a word in $F$ for $z$. Its dependency arrows can only disappear. Thus both lowered endpoints have triangular frames for the remaining $k-1$ auxiliaries. The projected edge is ordinary, or it is the identity if the erased pivot was the donor. Apply the induction hypothesis.

Otherwise the changed slot must be a pure pivot in both tuples, as every triangular frame has at least one pure pivot. If $r,r'$ solve the same auxiliary, use the first frame at the second vertex with $r$ replaced by $r'$. Its other $k-1$ rows are unchanged. Quotient by those rows. The quotient is $F*\langle t\rangle$ and the changed rows become one-auxiliary pivots there, as in the one-row exchange. The projected original step either remains a right multiplication or is the identity. Round 01's one-auxiliary path theorem applies.

Suppose $r$ solves $z$ and $r'$ solves a different auxiliary $t$. Keep the two slots of $r,q$ in the display. Put $C=(r,r',U)$ and $D=(r',r,U)$, with $U$ the common rows. The original tuple before the edge reaches $C$ by left-multiplying $q$ by $r$. The tuple after the edge reaches $D$ by replacing $q$ by $r'q^{-1}=r$, and $D$ is a permutation of $C$.

At the first endpoint every frame uses $r$ for $z$. Its donor $q$ has auxiliary exponent vector equal to the difference of those of $r'$ and $r$. The support of this vector is exactly $\{z,t\}$. Consequently, if $q$ is selected, it can be assigned only to $t$, since the $z$ assignment is already taken and a one-occurrence pivot has a nonzero exponent in its own variable. The same frame is valid at $C$ with $q$ replaced by the pure $t$ pivot $r'$. Project through $r$ first, as this pivot is pure. Then $q$ and $r'$ have exactly the same image. Thus the first endpoint and $C$ have equal projections for these frame choices. At the second endpoint the analogous frame uses $r'$ for $t$, and $q$ can only be selected for $z$. Replace it if selected by the pure $z$ pivot $r$ at $D$. After first projecting through $r'$, the image of $r$ is the inverse of the image of $q$. When $q$ is selected, its inversion has the same kernel and retraction. Otherwise it gives a single ordinary inversion in the final projection. The projections from $C,D$ are equivalent by the same-vertex exchange statement after permuting rows. This proves the edge case. Concatenate the projected paths. ∎

There is also a path criterion for arbitrary relative-basis frames. It suffices to select frames at adjacent endpoints with $k-1$ common unchanged rows, then connect the incoming and outgoing frame choices by successive one-row exchanges at each intermediate vertex. Killing the shared rows reduces justification of each edge to the one-auxiliary result. The triangular theorem is stronger than applying that particular criterion directly, because an edge can switch the only pure pivot to a different auxiliary without such shared unchanged rows.

## Exact examples and scope

In the following words capitals denote inverses. Let the base letters be $x,y$ and the auxiliaries $z,t$. The one-step right multiplication of the first row by the second is

```
(z, ZtztZT, zztx, ztty) -> (tztZT, ZtztZT, zztx, ztty).
```

The sole triangular frames use the first and third slots at the left and the first and fourth at the right. The first row cyclically changes from $z$ to $t$. The unchanged second row has three occurrences of each auxiliary, so it serves neither as a triangular pivot before the pure elimination nor as the second pivot in either frame. There is no shared unchanged triangular pivot slot across these frame choices. The intermediate tuple in the theorem is $(z,\mathrm{tztZT},\mathrm{zztx},\mathrm{ztty})$, whose first two rows project the others to $(x,y)$. The projections directly from the two endpoint frames are $(x^{-1},x^{-2}y)$ and $(y,y^{-2}x)$. They reach ordered $(x,y)$ in 5 and 11 strictly replayed rank-two moves respectively.

Relative-basis frames need not have connected one-row exchange graphs. Let $(U,V)$ be the basis of $F(z,t)$ obtained from $(z,t)$ by four alternating positive right Nielsen moves, starting with the first row. Then $U=\mathrm{zttzt}$ and $V=\mathrm{tztzttzt}$, with auxiliary exponent vectors $(2,3)$ and $(3,5)$. The tuple $(z,t,Ux,Vy)$ has relative-basis frames in slots $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$. For the second frame, compose the Nielsen automorphism on the auxiliary factor with appending $x,y$ to the images of the two generators. The four mixed pairs have auxiliary determinant of absolute value $2,3,3,5$. None can be a relative-basis frame, even allowing individual conjugations and inversions. This tuple normally generates $G$, since killing the first two rows makes the last two equal to $x,y$. Its two frame projections are both free bases, since the inverse Nielsen steps applied to $(x^{-1},y^{-1})$ give the projection of $(z,t)$ through the last two rows. The example excludes a general exchange-connectivity argument while leaving actual projected AC equivalence intact.

Another weaker condition allows a row to become a pivot only after *cancellation* under a prior substitution. For $(z,z x^{-1},[t,z]t y,y)$, with $[t,z]=tzt^{-1}z^{-1}$, choosing $z=1$ gives the new pivot $t y$ and finishes at $(x^{-1},y)$. Choosing the alternative pure pivot $z=x$ instead leaves $(x,[t,x]t y,y)$, with no cyclic one-occurrence pivot for $t$. The original tuple has no syntactic triangular frame, since the only row involving $t$ has three cyclic occurrences. Thus greedy recognition depends on the syntactic hypothesis.

## Consequences for the other tracks

At the padded starting tuple $(R,Z)$, choose the isolated auxiliary rows as the frame. At the full positive basis ending tuple, choose the same marked auxiliary basis letters wherever they occur. The theorem then compiles the entire padded section to an ordinary trivialization of $R$. Thus any normalized stable trivialization of a putative ordinary counterexample must contain a vertex that lacks a triangular frame relative to its fixed marked base and auxiliary letters. This applies for any padding size. [Peak-rank normalization](round-01-stable-disproof.md#fixed-rank-normalization).

If the endpoint is geometric, sequential cancellation of the primitive equations of any triangular frame gives a geometric projected tuple. For balanced trivial groups that projected tuple is ordinarily AC-trivial by the thickenable case. Thus geometry can be sought at arbitrary padded rank under the triangular-path hypothesis. The general ordinary conjecture and the existence of such an AK(3) path remain open. [Geometric descent](round-01-stable-proof.md#geometry-descends-through-a-defining-equation), [Lackenby, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06122v1).

## Paths with three crossing multiplications

Consider a normalized padded ordinary path to an unordered positive basis, as in the stable-disproof track. Absorb row swaps at the endpoint. Let a cut separate a nonempty subset of auxiliary rows from the other rows. Both blocks start with complementary coordinate spans in degree-one mod-two abelianization. If exactly three multiplications cross the cut and both directions occur, the directions must alternate. Exactly one generator from each block's initial coordinate basis then finishes in the opposite row block.

To prove this, call the first receiving block $A$, and the other block $B$. Before the first crossing their spans are their initial spaces $U,V$. At the first crossing $A$ becomes a nonzero graph over $U$. If the next two receivers are in $B$, that noncoordinate span of $A$ persists to the end. If the next receivers are $A$ and then $B$, throughout the first two crossings the donor block $B$ stays in $V$. The final span of $A$ still projects isomorphically onto $U$, so its being coordinate forces it to equal $U$. The last crossing then makes $B$ a nonzero graph over $V$, again impossible. Three crossings in the same direction can be removed by the one-direction projection in round 01 for an optimizing path. More generally, without the optimality assumption all three can point the same way. Thus whenever both directions occur, the receivers must be $A,B,A$. The unique update to $B$ uses an $A$ vector with nonzero projection to $U$. Hence the projection of the terminal $B$ span onto $U$ has rank one. The terminal span is coordinate and has dimension $\dim V$, so exactly one initial $U$ coordinate has replaced a $V$ coordinate. The complementary coordinate span of $A$ accounts for the opposite transfer. [Round 01 crossing bound](round-01-stable-disproof.md#necessary-relator-mixing-at-the-peak-rank).

**One incoming multiplication with one auxiliary.** Suppose there is only one padded pair and, after absorbing row swaps as reindexings, its row receives exactly one multiplication by another relator during the padded path. There may be arbitrarily many multiplications in the other direction. If the path ends at a permutation of the positive marked basis, the original tuple is ordinarily AC-trivial.

Before the sole incoming multiplication the auxiliary row $p$ is conjugate up to inversion to $z$, since its only updates have been inversions and conjugations. After that multiplication the same reasoning from the endpoint shows that the auxiliary row $p'$ is conjugate up to inversion to a single marked basis generator $a$. Project the first portion by $z=1$ and drop the auxiliary row, and project the last portion by $a=1$ and drop the same row. Each portion projects entirely to ordinary moves or identities in the free group on the surviving marked variables. At the transition write $p'=p q^{\pm1}$ with the same retained donor $q$ on both sides. If $a=z$, the two projections at the transition are identical. Otherwise $a\in X$. The donor projects to a conjugate of $a^{\pm1}$ when $z=1$, and to a conjugate of $z^{\pm1}$ when $a=1$. Identify the latter free group with the former by renaming its surviving $z$ to $a$ and fixing $X\setminus\{a\}$. Normalize both projected donors to $a$. Every other corresponding pair of projected words agrees after setting $a=1$, since both evaluations then kill the original $z,a$. Use the retained donor $a$ and the normal-closure correction from round 01 to match all other rows. This joins the two rank-$n$ projected portions. The argument also covers zero incoming multiplications by projecting the entire path by $z=1$.

The stable-disproof track applies the argument in either time direction and generalizes it to a whole auxiliary block. At the minimum feasible padding rank, each such block must receive at least two incoming multiplications and supply at least two outgoing multiplications. The conclusion uses no triangular-frame hypothesis. [Block elimination and move-count scope](round-02-stable-disproof.md).
