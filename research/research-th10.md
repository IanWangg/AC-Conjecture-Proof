## 1. Conventions

F = F(x,y), X = x⁻¹, Y = y⁻¹; π: F → Q an epimorphism, a = π(x), b = π(y); e(w) ∈ ℤ² the exponent vector. AC moves in Q: inversion, multiplication of one coordinate by the other or its inverse, conjugation of a coordinate by any element of Q, swap. "Multiply coordinate i by an element of the normal closure of the other coordinate" is a composite of AC moves (conjugate the donor, multiply, conjugate back). A pair (s,t) of Q is **admissible** if it normally generates Q and its image in Q_ab is Nielsen-equivalent to (ā, b̄); it is **standardizable** if (s,t) ~_Q (a,b). Q **has (AC)** if every admissible pair is standardizable; Q **obstructs** U if π(U) is not standardizable. If U normally generates F then π(U) is admissible (its exponent matrix is unimodular). A **class coordinate** of U is a coordinate of some pair AC-equivalent to U in F; conjugates and inverses of class coordinates are class coordinates. G_c := F/⟨⟨c⟩⟩.

## 2. Theorem A: the abelian-quotient filter (proved here)

**Lemma 0.** If (s,t) is admissible, there is a basis (p,q) of F with π(p) ≡ s and π(q) ≡ t modulo [Q,Q].
*Proof.* Some sequence of Nielsen moves takes (ā,b̄) to (s̄,t̄) in Q_ab. Apply the same sequence to (x,y) in F; the result is a basis (p,q), and its image in Q_ab is (s̄,t̄). ∎

**Theorem A.** Let (s,t) be an admissible pair of Q such that Q/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ is abelian. Then (s,t) ~_Q (a,b). Consequently, if some pair (c,w) ~_AC U in F has Q/⟨⟨π(c)⟩⟩ abelian, then Q does not obstruct U.

*Proof.* Take (p,q) as in Lemma 0.
1. Q/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ abelian means [Q,Q] ⊆ ⟨⟨s⟩⟩. Since t⁻¹π(q) ∈ [Q,Q] ⊆ ⟨⟨s⟩⟩, coordinate 1 may be multiplied by it: (s,t) ~ (s, π(q)).
2. Q/⟨⟨π(q)⟩⟩ is a quotient of F/⟨⟨q⟩⟩ ≅ ℤ (q is primitive), hence abelian, so [Q,Q] ⊆ ⟨⟨π(q)⟩⟩. Since s⁻¹π(p) ∈ [Q,Q], coordinate 0 may be multiplied by it: (s, π(q)) ~ (π(p), π(q)).
3. (p,q) is a basis of F, so it is carried to (x,y) by Nielsen moves, which are AC moves and commute with π: (π(p),π(q)) ~ (a,b).
For the last sentence: (π(c),π(w)) ~_Q π(U) is admissible. ∎

**Remarks.** (i) The case Q/⟨⟨π(c)⟩⟩ = 1 is H's Theorem 1 (and E's Prop. 5); H's Lemma 2.2 is the same two-step argument for a central kernel. The gain is that "abelian" is enough, so the filter applies to quotients with non-cyclic abelianization, where H's Theorem 1 "never applies". (ii) For Q = F the theorem says: a class coordinate c with G_c abelian (e.g. c primitive) makes U AC-trivial — the classical remark. (iii) The filter sees only whether ⟨⟨c⟩⟩·ker π ⊇ [F,F].

**Corollary A1 (criterion for (AC)).** Q has (AC) iff every admissible pair of Q is AC-equivalent to a pair one of whose coordinates s has Q/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ abelian. Q obstructs U iff no pair in the Q-class of π(U) has such a coordinate.
*Proof.* (⇒) (a,b) has Q/⟨⟨a⟩⟩ cyclic. (⇐) Admissibility is AC-invariant; apply Theorem A (after a swap if necessary). ∎

**Corollary A2 (two pairs).** If U and V each have a class coordinate whose one-relator quotient of Q is abelian, Q does not separate U from V. If ker π is φ-invariant (φ ∈ Aut(F)) and c is a class coordinate of U with Q/⟨⟨π(c)⟩⟩ abelian, then Q does not separate U from φ(U).
*Proof.* Both pairs are standardizable. For the second statement φ(c) is a class coordinate of φ(U) and the induced automorphism φ̄ of Q maps ⟨⟨π(c)⟩⟩ onto ⟨⟨π(φc)⟩⟩. ∎

## 3. Theorem B: torsion kills torus coordinates (proved here)

**Theorem B.** Let (u,v) be a basis of F, p, q nonzero integers, and suppose c = u^p v^{-q} (or a conjugate or the inverse of it) is a class coordinate of U. Let Q̄ = Q/⟨⟨π(c)⟩⟩. If the image of u in Q̄ has finite order prime to p, or the image of v has finite order prime to q — in particular if π(u) or π(v) has such an order in Q — then Q̄ is abelian and Q does not obstruct U.

*Proof.* In Q̄, z := ū^p = v̄^q commutes with ū and with v̄, which generate Q̄; so z is central. If ū has order m with gcd(m,p) = 1, choose k with kp ≡ 1 (mod m): ū = ū^{kp} = z^k is central, and Q̄ is generated by v̄ and a central element, hence abelian. The other case is symmetric. Apply Theorem A. ∎

**Torus coordinates used below** (each identity is checked by `experiments/th10_torus_coords.py --selftest`):
- braid relator: xyxy⁻¹x⁻¹y⁻¹ is conjugate to (xyx)²(xy)⁻³, and (xyx, xy) is a basis (x = v⁻¹u, y = x⁻¹v);
- x^n y^{-(n+1)}, basis (x,y);
- G1 for n = 3: the first relator of the target of `g_ak3_to_trefoil_t2_7.json` is conjugate to U²V⁻⁷ with (U,V) = (ψ((xy)³x), ψ(xy)), ψ: x ↦ xyx, y ↦ yx, a basis of F. (For general n, G's Prop. G1 gives the type (2,2n+1) with basis (ψ((xy)ⁿx), ψ(xy)): (xy)ⁿx·((yx)ⁿy)⁻¹ = vⁿxv^{-(n+1)}x = u·v^{-(2n+1)}·u for v = xy, u = vⁿx.)
- C4: x⁸y⁵ (basis (x,y)) and x⁴yx⁻¹y = x⁵(x⁻¹y)² (basis (x, x⁻¹y)) are coordinates of T(4;8,5) ~ C4 (`i_c4_to_T4_8_5.json`); xy³xy⁻² = w·y⁵·w with w = xy⁻² (basis (w,y)).

**Corollary B1 (the AK family).** Let n ≥ 1 and suppose Q obstructs AK(n). Then
(i) π(xyx) has infinite or even order, and π(xy) has infinite order or order divisible by 3;
(ii) π(x) has infinite order or order not prime to n, and π(y) infinite order or order not prime to n+1;
(iii) (using G1) π(U_n) has infinite or even order and π(V_n) infinite order or order not prime to 2n+1, where (U_n,V_n) = (ψ((xy)ⁿx), ψ(xy)).
In particular **no periodic group without elements of order 2, no periodic group without elements of order 3, hence no periodic p-group for any prime p and no group of exponent N with 6 ∤ N, obstructs any AK(n).** This covers every free Burnside group B(2,N) with N odd (the Novikov–Adian range) and every 2-generated Golod–Shafarevich, Grigorchuk or Gupta–Sidki type torsion p-group, residually finite or not.
*Proof.* Theorem B for the three coordinates. ∎

**Corollary B2 (AK(3): every primitive element).** If Q obstructs AK(3), then for every primitive element g of F the order of π(g) is infinite or divisible by 42. Consequently: a group of exponent N obstructs AK(3) only if 42 ∣ N; a quotient of the triangle group Δ(l,m,k) = ⟨x,y | x^l, y^m, (xy)^k⟩ obstructs AK(3) only if 42 divides l, m and k; if all primitive elements have orders dividing m in Q and Q obstructs AK(3), then 42 ∣ m and the universal such quotient PB(m) := F/⟨⟨g^m : g primitive⟩⟩ obstructs AK(3).
*Proof.* The AC class of AK(3) is closed under Aut(F₂) (D, Lemma D6), so for every basis (u,v) of F the words u²v⁻³, u³v⁻⁴, u²v⁻⁷ are class coordinates of AK(3) (apply the automorphism taking the three bases above to (u,v)). A primitive g is the first member of a basis and the second member of another one. Theorem B with u²v⁻³ and u = g gives "even or infinite"; with u²v⁻³, v = g: "divisible by 3 or infinite"; with u²v⁻⁷, v = g: "divisible by 7 or infinite". x, y, xy are primitive; a quotient with all primitive orders dividing m is a quotient of PB(m), and obstruction passes to larger quotients. ∎

**Corollary B3 (chirality targets).** Let ker π be characteristic and let m(Q) ∈ ℕ ∪ {∞} be the common order of the images of the primitive elements ("primitive order"). If Q separates C4 from the standard pair or from any φ(C4), φ ∈ Aut(F₂), then m(Q) is infinite or divisible by 10. For C3 = T(2;4,7): infinite or divisible by 210. For a quotient with arbitrary kernel: Q does not obstruct T(a;p,q) = (x^p y^q, x^a y x⁻¹ y) if π(x) has finite order prime to p or to a+1, or π(y) finite order prime to q, or π(x⁻¹y) finite odd order.
*Proof.* Corollary A2 and Theorem B with the coordinates listed above; x^a y x⁻¹ y = x^{a+1}(x⁻¹y)². For C4 the types are (8,5), (5,2), (2,5): a finite m must be even and divisible by 5. For C3 the certificates starting at C3 contain coordinates of torus types (2,3), (4,7), (5,9) (experiment, §7), which force 2, 3, 5, 7 ∣ m. ∎

So for periodic quotients the first primitive orders that survive are 42 (AK(3)), 10 (C4), 210 (C3): nothing in the classical odd-exponent Burnside world.

**Proposition A3 (primitive coset; proved here).** Let 𝒢_Q := {g ∈ Q : Q/⟨⟨g⟩⟩ is abelian}; it contains the image of every primitive element of F. If (s,t) is admissible and t ∈ g·⟨⟨s⟩⟩ for some g ∈ 𝒢_Q, then (s,t) is standardizable. Example: if Q/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ ≅ S₃, then (s,t) is standardizable.
*Proof.* t⁻¹g ∈ ⟨⟨s⟩⟩, so (s,t) ~ (s,g), which is admissible with g ∈ 𝒢_Q; apply Theorem A. For S₃: the image of t normally generates S₃, so it is a transposition; ā, b̄ generate S₃, so one of them is a transposition; transpositions are conjugate, so t ≡ h⁻¹π(x)h or h⁻¹π(y)h modulo ⟨⟨s⟩⟩, the image of a primitive element. ∎

By Corollary A1, **Q obstructs U iff no coordinate of any pair in the Q-class of π(U) lies in 𝒢_Q.** An obstruction is therefore exactly an AC-invariant set of admissible pairs of Q that contains π(U) and avoids the locus 𝒢_Q in both coordinates.

## 4. Free Burnside groups B = B(2,N) = F/F^N

**External facts used (recalled, not verified here).** (X1) Novikov–Adian, Adian: for odd N ≥ 665, B(2,N) is infinite and the centralizer of every nontrivial element is cyclic. (X2) Kostrikin, Zelmanov, with the Hall–Higman reduction: B(2,N) has a largest finite quotient R(2,N). (X3) Ivanov, Lysenok: B(2,N) is infinite for large N divisible by a high power of 2; its finite subgroups are then not cyclic in general.

**4.1 Filter by filter (N odd ≥ 665).**
- H Theorem 1 never applies: B_ab = (ℤ/N)² is not cyclic.
- H Theorem 2(i): B has no nontrivial abelian normal subgroup (proved from X1): such an A lies in the centralizer of one of its nontrivial elements, so A is finite cyclic; B/C_B(A) embeds in Aut(A), and C_B(A) is cyclic, so B would be finite.
- H Corollary 3: B is not virtually solvable (a finitely generated virtually solvable torsion group is finite).
- H Theorem 2(ii): whether B has a (necessarily infinite, nonabelian) minimal normal subgroup is **not decided here** (open for me); Proposition C below shows that the top chief factors of the finite residual are invisible.
- N2.1: F^N is verbal, so every automorphism of F acts on B; B is basis-sensitive in N's sense.
- **Filter A: B fails it** for every pair with a torus class coordinate of type (p,q) unless gcd(N,p) > 1 and gcd(N,q) > 1 (Theorem B): AK(n) needs 6 ∣ N, AK(3) needs 42 ∣ N, C4 needs 10 ∣ N, C3 needs 210 ∣ N. So **B(2,N), N odd, obstructs nothing in the AK or T(a;p,q) families and separates none of them from an automorphic image.** For 42 ∣ N the three torus quotients of AK(3) are nonabelian (they map onto S₃, S₃, D₇ respectively; self-test), so filter A does not decide B(2,42k); these groups (X3) are not covered by X1.

**Proposition C (finite residual; proved here from X2).** Let B = B(2,N) be infinite and K = ker(B → R(2,N)). Then: (a) K is the finite residual of B, K ⊆ [B,B], K is finitely generated, infinite, perfect, and has no proper subgroup of finite index; (b) K has maximal proper B-normal subgroups M; for each, K/M is a nonabelian minimal normal subgroup of B/M, and B/M has (AC); the same holds for B/(M₁ ∩ … ∩ M_r); (c) hence neither an obstruction nor a failure of (AC) in B is visible in any of these quotients.
*Proof.* (a) Every finite quotient of B factors through R(2,N), and R(2,N) maps onto (ℤ/N)². K has finite index in a finitely generated group. K/[K,K] is a finitely generated abelian group of exponent dividing N, hence finite; [K,K] is characteristic in K, so normal of finite index in B, so it contains K. A finite-index subgroup of K contains a finite-index normal subgroup of B, hence K. (b) A union of a chain of proper B-normal subgroups of K is proper because K is finitely generated; Zorn gives M. K/M is minimal normal in B/M, perfect and nontrivial, hence nonabelian. (B/M)/(K/M) = R(2,N) is finite and has (AC) (H Cor. 3(b)); H Theorem 2(ii) lifts (AC) to B/M. For distinct M₁, M₂: M₁M₂ = K, so M₁/(M₁∩M₂) ≅ K/M₂ as B-groups is a nonabelian minimal normal subgroup of B/(M₁∩M₂) with quotient B/M₁; induct. (c) follows. ∎

**Lemma E (Euclid for torus pairs; classical, proof included).** If ad − bc = ±1, then (x^a y^b, x^c y^d) is AC-trivial in F.
*Proof.* u₀u₁⁻¹ = x^a y^{b−d} x^{−c} is conjugate to x^{a−c}y^{b−d}: the row operation "row₀ − row₁" is realized inside the family. Replacing u₁ first by x^{−c}y^{−d} (a conjugate of u₁⁻¹) and restoring it afterwards realizes "row₀ + row₁"; symmetrically for row₁. These operations reduce a unimodular matrix to a signed permutation matrix, i.e. the pair to (x^{±1}, y^{±1}) or (y^{±1}, x^{±1}). ∎

**Proposition D (power twists; proved here).** Let π(x) = a, π(y) = b have orders dividing N in Q, and kl ≡ 1 (mod N). Then (a^k, b^l) ~_Q (a,b). If Q is relatively free of exponent N, let P ≤ Aut(Q) be generated by the tame automorphisms (induced from Aut(F)) and the power automorphisms θ_k: a ↦ a^k, b ↦ b (gcd(k,N) = 1), and det: P → (ℤ/N)^×/{±1} the determinant on Q_ab. Then every α ∈ P with det α = 1 satisfies (α(a), α(b)) ~_Q (a,b).
*Proof.* Let k' be an inverse of k modulo N²; then k' ≡ l (mod N) and kk' = 1 + N²β. The pair (x^k y^N, x^{Nβ} y^{k'}) has determinant 1, so it is AC-trivial in F (Lemma E), and its image in Q is (a^k, b^l). For the second statement let S = {α ∈ Aut(Q): α(a,b) ~ (a,b)}; S is a subgroup (automorphisms preserve ~_Q) containing the tame automorphisms T. P/⟨⟨T⟩⟩_P is generated by the images of the θ_k and det identifies it with (ℤ/N)^×/{±1} (θ₋₁ is tame), so ker det = ⟨⟨T⟩⟩_P, which is generated by the conjugates θ_kτθ_k⁻¹, τ a Nielsen generator. θ_kτθ_k⁻¹ ∈ S iff τ(a^l, b) := (τ(a)^l, τ(b)) ~ (a^l, b), l = k⁻¹. For τ an inversion this is an inversion move; for τ: a ↦ ab it holds since (ab)^l ≡ a^l mod ⟨⟨b⟩⟩; for τ: b ↦ ba since ba ≡ b mod ⟨⟨a⟩⟩ = ⟨⟨a^l⟩⟩; for the swap, (b^l, a) ~ (a, b^l), and applying the automorphism b ↦ b^l to (a^l, b^k) ~ (a,b) gives (a^l, b) ~ (a, b^l). ∎

So the obvious non-tame bases of B(2,N) are not counterexamples to (AC) for B(2,N).

**4.2 The Burnside AC problem (open).** *Does B(2,N) have (AC)?* By Corollary A1 this fails iff some admissible AC class of B consists of pairs (s,t) with both B/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ and B/⟨⟨t⟩⟩ nonabelian. For N = p prime these one-relator Burnside quotients E = B/⟨⟨s⟩⟩ are of two kinds only (proved here): E ≅ ℤ/p, or E is infinite with E' perfect of index p. (E_ab ≅ ℤ/p because s̄ is part of a basis of (ℤ/p)²; E/E'' is a finitely generated metabelian torsion group, hence a finite p-group with cyclic abelianization, hence cyclic, so E' = E''; a finite E is nilpotent, so E' = 1.) A failure of (AC) in B would *not* by itself give a counterexample to the AC conjecture: the offending pair must be the image of a pair normally generating F, and for the pairs of this project it is not (Corollary B1).
